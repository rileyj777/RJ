# Style, method, and example bank

Read this before drafting the first email in a session. It holds the matching
method, the two North Star exemplars, five annotated examples, a 42-card bank
of live matches off the board, and the conversion patterns from deals that
advanced.

---

## The six-step matching process

This is the loop that turns a prospect into a capability-to-bottleneck email.

1. **Open on a real trigger.** A specific, verifiable signal: an operational
   milestone (pilot commissioned, first unit, maiden field survey), a closed
   round, a federal program or prize (DOE ESRA Prize, DPA, ARPA-E), an
   accelerator or cohort selection (Silicon Catalyst, HAX, BHP Think and Act
   Differently, Rice Alliance), or a named partnership. No trigger, no email.
2. **Locate the physical bottleneck.** Read past the press release into the
   actual technology and find the hard mechanical, thermal, fluid, or
   structural problem their approach creates right now. This is the whole game:
   not "you do X," but "X creates this specific engineering problem." A
   room-temperature exothermic metal-water reaction is a two-phase
   thermal-management problem. A subsea nodule collector is pressure housings
   plus a buoyancy engine plus depth. A 900C thermal battery is thermal cycling
   and stress on a ceramic vessel.
3. **Map a named capability to that bottleneck.** Match the specific JE tool
   (combustion or thermal CFD, structural or fatigue FEA, pressure-vessel
   design, electrochemical cell-and-stack build, materials of construction,
   DFM, skid packaging) to the specific bottleneck, and name the sub-problems in
   their language.
4. **Frame the offer as core, around it, or both.** "We can help with the core
   reactor itself, the plant around it, or both." Never "stay clear of your
   core."
5. **Set the register by type and size.** A pre-hardware startup gets capacity
   to cut prototype cycles. A peer with real in-house engineering gets surge or
   overflow framing, explicitly not a suggestion they need help. A large or
   established player gets supplemental capacity on specific programs. A
   software, biology, or materials-core company gets an explicit
   acknowledgment, then JE positioned around the hardware.
6. **Close with the right ask.** A named engineering contact gets a fair-read
   validation ("Does that track? Is the reactor or the separation the tighter
   constraint?"). No named contact gets a routing question ("point me toward
   whoever owns engineering"). Size the proof line (30-plus DOE programs, the
   Spokane shop, 800A of 480V, build-and-ship) to the prospect.

## The two North Star exemplars

Write in the style of these two Andy-starred messages. The defining move in
both is a creative, researched fit hypothesis: study the prospect's actual
technology, cite specific proof (a named program, material system, technical
constraint, or recent corporate event), then describe the concrete engineering
path JE would take. Go specific and technical. This replaces any generic "for a
company doing X that could include..." scaffolding.

- **The I-Pulse / Spielman warm follow-up** (warm/personalized register). Its
  hallmarks: bold and underline the key lines so the message survives a
  skim-before-delete; lead with proof of research by citing a specific named
  corporate event or technical detail; include honest epistemic humility ("I am
  partly guessing where the seams are"); and use an "Is this a fair read?"
  validation line that invites correction.
- **The Ames Lab / Andrew Kustas cold email** (cold register). Its hallmark is
  the same researched fit hypothesis done cold: a named lab program, the real
  material or process constraint, and the concrete engineering path, closing
  with a cold-variant validation ("does that track with where you're hitting
  walls?").

## Five annotated examples

**Read these for the bottleneck-to-capability MOVE only.** Their subject lines,
their "Best, Riley / Johnston Engineering" sign-offs, and their "interested in
learning more about your development priorities" closing sentence are
**outdated** and violate the current editorial rules in `SKILL.md`. Use them to
study how the capabilities paragraph is scoped to one real engineering problem,
then write the current-form subject, no sign-off, and a soft-curious close.

### Example 1 — Hephae Energy Technology (geothermal / ultra-high-temperature downhole tools)
**Lesson: find the hook where the prospect describes the exact problem JE solves, and lead with it.**

> Hi Steve,
>
> I came across Hephae's work on ultra high temperature drilling tools and
> noticed the Stanford Geothermal Workshop presentation on thermal management
> analysis of the multi-PCB-stack configuration for geothermal drilling tools.
> That is precisely the kind of problem we work on.
>
> [Capabilities paragraph, scoped to the Pandora210 MWD system pushing
> directional drilling beyond the 200 degree barrier: thermal FEA and CFD in
> ANSYS to model heat dissipation and temperature distribution in the
> multi-PCB-stack geometry under downhole thermal loading; electronics packaging
> and thermal interface design to hold component performance at ultra-high
> temperature; materials selection and structural analysis for the tool housing
> and electronic assembly under combined thermal and mechanical loads; and
> custom test fixtures for thermal characterization and qualification of the PCB
> stack and sensor packages before field trials. Involved through fabrication
> and testing.]

Why it works: their engineer presented at Stanford on exactly JE's core
capability. "That is precisely the kind of problem we work on" lands because it
is true. When you find a hook like this, lead with it and let it carry the
email.

### Example 2 — DISA Technologies (critical minerals / High-Pressure Slurry Ablation)
**Lesson: operational milestones beat everything, and precise process physics proves you understand it.**

> Hi John,
>
> I came across DISA's recent milestones, including the first 100-ton-per-hour
> commercial HPSA unit sold for Eagle Mine's nickel and copper concentrator
> circuit and the first-of-its-kind NRC license for abandoned uranium mine
> remediation.
>
> [Capabilities paragraph describing HPSA in its own mechanics — ore and waste
> slurry pumped through opposing high-pressure nozzles into a high-energy impact
> zone — then: CFD of slurry flow distribution and pressure drop through the
> nozzle and impact-zone geometry; structural and wear FEA of nozzle assemblies
> and impact-zone components under high-pressure abrasive conditions; fluid
> handling and slurry-circuit hardware for higher-capacity next-gen units; and
> modular mobile-unit design and fabrication coordination for remediation
> deployments across diverse sites.]

Why it works: two verified operational milestones in the hook, then the process
described in the prospect's own language. A COO reads it and knows you did the
homework.

### Example 3 — Daanaa Resolution (power electronics / solar substring inverters)
**Lesson: name the specific products, and map the capabilities paragraph to the exact engineering challenge the product creates.**

> Hi Javad,
>
> I noticed your recent announcement about the Zodiac Solar substring inverter
> samples.
>
> [Capabilities paragraph naming the Virgo and Pandora modules and the one hard
> problem a panel-integrated inverter creates — cramming power electronics onto
> the back of a PV module that bakes in the sun and thermal-cycles daily:
> thermal architecture and FEA under solar irradiance and outdoor cycling;
> electronics packaging for the density constraints of the panel-integrated form
> factor; structural analysis of enclosure and mounting hardware under
> mechanical load and thermal expansion; DFM for reliable volume manufacture;
> and test fixtures with simulation correlation. Names die-level thermal mapping,
> TIM interface characterization, hotspot suppression, conjugate heat transfer.]

Why it works: it names the actual product line and modules from their
announcement, and the capabilities are scoped to the single hard problem the
product creates, not generic thermal talk. A CTO recognizes a peer.

### Example 4 — Endolith (biomining / AI-guided microbial copper recovery)
**Lesson: when the core is software, biology, or chemistry, say so out loud and position JE around it.**

> Hi Liz,
>
> I came across Endolith's work on AI-guided microbial copper recovery,
> including the BHP TAD milestone showing your microbes outperform conventional
> heap leaching.
>
> [Acknowledgment sentence: "I recognize that Endolith's core platform is
> biology and AI, not hardware, but the physical infrastructure surrounding the
> bioleaching process matters equally to field performance." Then the real
> hardware a biology company still has to build and deploy: custom biohatchery
> equipment and fluid-distribution hardware for consistent inoculation across
> heap-leach surface areas; process equipment for the microbial diagnostic and
> monitoring systems; ANSYS CFD and FEA to validate inoculation distribution and
> fluid handling before field deployment; and modular, field-deployable
> equipment for rapid install with minimal site disruption.]

Why it works: the acknowledgment sentence disarms the "why is a mechanical
engineering firm in my inbox" reaction instantly and reframes JE as the
complement, then points at the real hardware.

### Example 5 — Fervo Energy (geothermal, large public company)
**Lesson: for big companies, reframe from "capability gap" to "supplemental capacity," and handle a funding/IPO hook with congratulations and no dollar figures.**

> Hi Koda,
>
> Congratulations on Fervo's Nasdaq IPO this week.
>
> [Supplemental-capacity register throughout: "supplemental engineering
> capacity," "additional structural and thermal FEA capacity on specific
> downhole component or wellhead hardware programs," "discrete hardware programs
> where additional outside capacity helps capital program timelines." Still
> specific with real programs — the 400 MW Cape Station project in Utah, fiber
> optic monitoring, advanced well completion — so it does not read as generic.]

Why it works: for a multi-billion-dollar public company the register shifts from
"you have a gap" to "we can absorb overflow on specific programs." The IPO hook
is one line of congratulations with no raise amount.

## What converts after the reply (Patterns A-E)

The cold first-touch only has to earn the reply and the intro call. But write it
knowing this motion follows, so the tone you set (technical, specific,
build-focused) is the tone that carries to a priced proposal.

**Pattern A — pain-first cold open earns the fastest yes.** The highest-
converting cold open in the data was not a capabilities intro. It was one
specific engineering-pain question with a tiny ask. Recovered Potential (in
Negotiation, ~$580K) opened with a subject naming the outcome the prospect wants
("Optimizing Wastewater Solutions for Industry"), one concrete question ("are
you looking into ways to improve nitrogen recovery efficiency in wastewater
systems?"), and a low-friction ask ("a quick 15-minute chat"). She accepted the
same day. When you know the specific technical pain, lead with the question, not
the resume.

**Pattern B — database/map-sourced open, name where you found them.** GlycoSurf
and Phinix both got warm replies from an open that cited the public database
they were listed in ("I saw your organization listed on the Critical Minerals &
Materials Projects Map Database..."; "I came across Phinix from the Critical
Mineral Database and Map and was intrigued by your work in the DOE project on
low-concentration precious metal recovery using solvent extraction."). Dr. Das
replied to book a Zoom. When the lead came from a named database, accelerator, or
map, say so in the first sentence.

**Pattern C — when the prospect corrects the fit, pivot, do not push.**
GlycoSurf replied that they are a specialty-chemicals supplier, not a primary DOE
proposer. Riley pivoted rather than forcing the pitch: acknowledged the
correction, then reframed around their chemical production methods and the custom
machinery they use or may want, closing on an intro meeting. That turned a "we're
not the buyer you think" into a booked meeting and a referral relationship. Same
"acknowledge their core, position JE around it" move as Endolith, done live.

**Pattern D — answer qualifying questions with named proof points and hard
numbers.** One Scientific (in Negotiation, ~$410K) replied with three qualifying
questions: in-house fabrication and pilot hosting? early TRL 4-6 experience?
engagement structures? Every answer was backed by a named project with a number:
JE engineered, built, and hosts/tests the Capture6 pilot (supported their raise)
and is expanding its fab; most projects fall in TRL 4-6 across 29 DOE-funded
clean-tech projects in two years; JE built OCOchem's CO2-to-formate pilot,
running continuously at ~60 tons/year, scaled up twice; engagement is design,
analysis, build, test, end-to-end or targeted. When a prospect is testing whether
you can actually build, generic capability language loses and named projects with
throughput numbers win.

> *prospect's* funding figures in a hook. Citing what Capture6 or OCOchem
> delivered is fair game and it converts.

**Pattern E — the conversion motion after the first meeting.** Every deal that
advanced followed the same rhythm: (1) intro call, then a same-day recap with a
clean numbered action-items list; (2) NDA fast, either party's paper; (3)
technical interchange with **Andy Johnston, P.E.** engaging directly, engineer to
engineer, which is what actually moved the deals; (4) ask for the PFD / process
model with flow rates, the artifact Andy needs to scope and price; (5) pricing
estimate, then proposal, then negotiation.
